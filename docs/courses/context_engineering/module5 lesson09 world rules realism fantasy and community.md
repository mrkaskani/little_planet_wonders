**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

# 1. Learning objective

- Define what is possible in the story world.
- Use fantasy without confusing real-world safety.
- Create a respectful community context.
- Draft the World Rules section of the World Bible.

---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

A recurring world needs internal logic. If animals speak in one episode but not another, if a small character can lift a heavy object only when convenient, or if fantasy repeatedly overrides real safety behavior, children and creators lose a stable frame of reference.

World rules also affect continuity and prompt quality. They determine how locations function, which characters know one another, what adults do, how transportation works, how weather behaves, and which imaginative elements are allowed. Clear rules help a series remain playful without accidentally teaching that real animals, roads, water, tools, or strangers behave like fantasy elements.


## Recurring example series

Throughout these lessons, examples come from **Lumi and Pip’s Little Garden**, a gentle animated series for children approximately two to five, designed primarily for ages three to four.

- **Lumi:** a curious four-year-old child who learns by observing, asking questions, and trying again.
- **Pip:** a small teal garden bird who notices patterns and invites audience participation.
- **Auntie Noura:** a calm adult guide who models safe behavior without taking over every problem.
- **Main locations:** the Rainbow Garden, the Cozy Workshop, and the Little Pond Path.
- **Visual identity:** rounded 2D cut-paper shapes, soft texture, readable silhouettes, simple backgrounds, warm daylight, and stable eye-level cameras.
- **Audio identity:** short dialogue, warm voices, restrained music, gentle Foley, quiet observation space, and no sudden loud sounds.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. World premise

A short description of where the series takes place, who belongs there, and what kind of stories naturally occur.

## 2. Fantasy level

Choose a consistent level: mostly real with one fantasy character, fully talking-animal world, magical objects, or imaginative play. The example world is mostly realistic, while Pip can speak.

## 3. Safety boundary between fantasy and reality

Fantasy must not erase safety. Pip may speak, but ordinary birds should not be approached or held because Pip is friendly. A magical path does not remove water, road, or tool rules.

## 4. Cause and effect

Actions should produce understandable results. Water helps a dry plant; a ball rolls down a slope; a mismatched basket remains mismatched. Avoid arbitrary outcomes that weaken learning.

## 5. Community roles

Define children, caregivers, neighbors, teachers, workers, and visitors respectfully. Adults support safety without controlling every discovery.

## 6. Cultural respect

Names, clothing, homes, food, music, accents, and family structures should be represented thoughtfully and reviewed by relevant humans when specificity is introduced.

## 7. Character knowledge boundary

Define what characters already know and what they are still learning. Auntie Noura may know garden safety; Lumi and Pip should not suddenly know every answer before the learning process.

## 8. World-scale problem rule

Most stories should remain local and manageable. A misplaced ball, dry plant, or turn-taking problem fits the world better than a city-wide emergency.

## 9. Continuity rules

Record recurring landmarks, travel time, location relationships, weather logic, community routines, and whether objects or characters can move between locations.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

### World premise

“Sunseed Garden is a small community garden beside a calm neighborhood. Children visit with trusted adults to explore colors, shapes, plants, sounds, routines, and relationships. The world follows familiar real-life cause and effect. Pip is a speaking bird, but ordinary animals remain real animals and must be treated with space and care.”

This premise protects both imagination and real-world safety.

# 6. Reusable prompt template

Create World Rules for a recurring animated preschool series.

Use the approved Series Bible, audience profile, safety bibles, educational philosophy, and emotional promise.

- world premise;
- fantasy level;
- allowed imaginative elements;
- real-world safety rules fantasy cannot override;

# 7. Weak prompt

> The garden is magical, so anything can happen whenever the story needs it.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- No stable world logic exists.
- Arbitrary magic can remove educational cause and effect.
- Safety rules may change for convenience.
- Character abilities and knowledge become inconsistent.
- Continuity across episodes becomes impossible to track.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Sunseed Garden follows familiar real-world cause and effect. Plants need water and time; objects fall, roll, get wet, or become dirty in understandable ways. Pip can speak and participate like a friend, but ordinary animals remain real animals.

Small imaginative moments may support wonder, such as a gentle musical glow when a match is found, but magic cannot solve the main learning problem, remove safety boundaries, or change character identity. Adults remain available for risk-sensitive situations, and most problems stay local and solvable.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Using magic to solve every problem.
2. Changing world rules for one scene.
3. Making adults absent whenever danger creates drama.
4. Giving characters knowledge only because the script needs it.
5. Treating community members as stereotypes.
6. Ignoring travel, weather, and location continuity.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Write your World Rules.

1. Write the world premise.
2. Choose the fantasy level.
3. List three allowed imaginative elements.
4. List five real-world safety rules fantasy cannot override.
5. Write three cause-and-effect rules.
6. Define adult support and character knowledge.
7. Define the normal problem scale.
8. Add cultural-review and continuity requirements.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

Why define fantasy level?

### Answer

To keep world behavior, safety, and expectations consistent.

### Question 2

Can magic replace the main learning process?

### Answer

Usually no; the characters should observe, try, and learn.

### Question 3

Why define character knowledge?

### Answer

To prevent characters from knowing answers inconsistently.

### Question 4

What problem scale fits the example series?

### Answer

Local, mild, visible, and solvable.

### Question 5

Can fantasy override water or road safety?

### Answer

No.
