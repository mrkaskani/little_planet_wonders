# Module 20

## Lesson 39 of 72 — Writing Short, Concrete, Character-Specific Dialogue

**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes  
**Estimated practice time:** 15 minutes

---

# 1. Learning objective

- Write dialogue that children under five can understand.
- Give each character a distinct but consistent speech identity.
- Use short sentences, concrete vocabulary, repetition, and pauses.
- Avoid sarcasm, lectures, complex jokes, and overloaded lines.
- Create a Final Dialogue Script.

---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Dialogue must do more than sound pleasant. It should communicate story, learning, emotion, relationship, and participation without overloading the child.

Young children benefit from short, concrete sentences connected to visible action. Long speeches may be grammatically correct but difficult to process. Characters should sound different, but voice distinction should come from word choice, rhythm, curiosity, warmth, and role—not extreme accents or caricature.

Final dialogue also determines later voice generation and speaking-video timing. The script should be carefully reviewed before exact TTS begins.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Concrete language

Use words connected to visible objects, actions, places, and emotions. Replace “Let us evaluate the available possibilities” with “Let’s look at the two baskets.”

## 2. One idea per sentence

A sentence should normally communicate one main idea. Split explanation, emotion, and instruction into separate lines.

## 3. Short exchanges

Several short turns are often easier to understand than one long speech.

## 4. Character speech identity

Lumi asks direct curious questions. Pip uses short pattern-noticing phrases. Auntie Noura uses calm guidance and reassurance.

## 5. Repetition with variation

Repeat important words naturally while changing the sentence or action.

## 6. Visible reference

Dialogue should refer to what the child can currently see. Avoid discussing several unseen objects or past events during a learning moment.

## 7. No sarcasm

Young children may interpret sarcasm literally. Use direct sincere meaning.

## 8. No humiliation humor

Do not make mistakes, names, voices, bodies, families, accents, or abilities the joke.

## 9. Dialogue and silence

Characters do not need to speak continuously. Allow action, expression, ambience, and observation to carry meaning.

## 10. Exact wording

Once approved for speaking production, every word matters. Do not treat dialogue as a loose suggestion.

## 11. Pronunciation readiness

Mark names, learning words, unusual terms, emphasis, and pauses before TTS.

## 12. Read-aloud test

Read the dialogue aloud slowly. Remove adult explanations, repeated filler, difficult phrasing, and unnecessary words.

The improved dialogue uses visible meaning, short turns, and a clear next action.

# 7. Weak prompt

> Write clever, fast, funny dialogue with long explanations, sarcasm, jokes adults will appreciate, and lots of character interruptions.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- Fast overlapping speech reduces comprehension.
- Long explanations overload memory.
- Sarcasm may be interpreted literally.
- Adult jokes may distract from the child’s experience.
- Interruptions remove response and observation time.
- Dialogue purpose is not connected to the learning goal.
- Character voice may become inconsistent.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Write short sincere dialogue for children approximately three to four.

Use one idea per sentence. Let Lumi speak in warm curious questions, Pip in short pattern-noticing phrases, and Auntie Noura in calm supportive guidance.

Repeat “red,” “match,” and “basket” only when connected to visible action. Include quiet pauses. Remove sarcasm, adult jokes, overlapping speech, lectures, and unnecessary explanation.

Treat the approved wording as final for later exact dialogue generation.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Writing adult-style explanation.
2. Using narration-like dialogue.
3. Letting characters interrupt constantly.
4. Repeating vocabulary mechanically.
5. Adding jokes that require advanced understanding.
6. Using sarcasm.
7. Leaving placeholders in the final script.
8. Changing wording during voice generation.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Write one page of final dialogue.

1. Use one scene from your Episode Breakdown.
2. Write exact lines.
3. Limit each sentence to one idea.
4. Distinguish Lumi, Pip, and Auntie Noura.
5. Repeat the target word naturally.
6. Insert one participation pause.
7. Add emotion and pronunciation notes.
8. Read aloud and remove at least twenty percent of unnecessary words.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

Why use one idea per sentence?

### Answer

To reduce processing load and improve clarity.
### Question 2

Can preschool dialogue use sarcasm?

### Answer

It should generally avoid sarcasm.
### Question 3

What makes dialogue character-specific?

### Answer

Stable word choice, rhythm, role, and emotional behavior.
### Question 4

Why write pauses?

### Answer

They affect participation, performance, timing, and video duration.
### Question 5

When does dialogue become exact?

### Answer

Once approved for final voice production.
