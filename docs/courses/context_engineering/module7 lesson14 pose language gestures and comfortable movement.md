# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Body language often communicates before dialogue. A clear pose can show looking, thinking, listening, asking, showing, helping, or celebrating. If a pose is too complicated, asymmetrical, fast, or physically extreme, it may become difficult to generate consistently and difficult for a child to understand.

Movement is also an imitation concern. Characters should model safe, calm, purposeful actions. This lesson creates a limited vocabulary of approved poses and gestures that can be reused across episodes and referenced in prompts.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Pose as a story statement

A strong pose communicates one idea. Lumi holding the red ball with both hands says “look at this.” Pip leaning slightly forward with a head tilt says “I am curious.”

## 2. Line of action

The main directional flow through the body should be simple and readable. Extreme bends or twists are unnecessary for preschool performance.

## 3. Open and closed posture

Open posture can communicate welcome or reassurance. Slightly closed posture may show thinking or mild concern. Avoid aggressive looming or threatening expansion.

## 4. Gesture vocabulary

Define recurring gestures: point, show, listen, think, ask, help, wait, celebrate, and try again. Reuse them consistently.

## 5. Gesture size

Small gestures suit close-ups; medium gestures suit wider shots. Large gestures should be reserved for clear songs or demonstrations and remain comfortable.

## 6. Hand clarity

Hands are common generation failure points. Use simple hand poses, avoid overlapping fingers, avoid hands near faces during speaking unless necessary, and review every frame.

## 7. Safe movement

Walking, turning, reaching, lifting, and placing should be slow enough to read. Avoid running, jumping, spinning, collisions, or balancing unless specifically safe and supervised.

## 8. Inclusive participation

When inviting movement, offer alternatives: perform, point, imagine, or watch. Do not require standing, jumping, or precise coordination.

## 9. One movement per segment

A segment should normally contain one primary movement: reach, pick up, turn, show, or place. Several movements should become A→B, B→C, C→D.

## 10. Movement continuity

Track body orientation, foot position, hand occupancy, prop holder, screen direction, and ending pose. Shared boundary frames support chained motion.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

## Showing pose

Lumi stands at eye level with the camera.

- Feet stable.
- Torso facing slightly toward the audience.
- Red ball held with both hands at chest height.
- Elbows relaxed.
- Eyes move from the ball to the audience.
- Gentle smile.
- No extra pointing while both hands hold the prop.

This pose clearly communicates “look at the red ball” and protects prop continuity.

# 6. Reusable prompt template

Create a pose or movement direction for [CHARACTER].

**Permanent context**  
Approved Character Bible, Expression Guide, safety rules, camera language, and preschool movement limits.

**Current action**  
[One primary action]

**Starting state**  
[Pose, orientation, hands, prop, expression]

**Ending state**  
[Pose, orientation, hands, prop, expression]

Define:
- body line;
- feet or base position;
- torso direction;
- hand or wing placement;
- head and eye direction;
- gesture size;
- movement speed;
- one camera behavior.

**Elements allowed to change**  
Only the body parts required for the action.

**Elements that must remain unchanged**  
Identity, clothing, prop appearance, location, camera perspective, lighting, and nonmoving body elements.

**Safety requirements**  
No unsafe reaching, jumping, spinning, collision, abrupt direction change, or imitable risky movement.

**Output requirements**  
One readable pose or one controlled movement.

# 7. Weak prompt

> Lumi waves, jumps, spins, points, laughs, turns around, and throws the ball into the basket.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- Too many actions are combined.
- Jumping and spinning may be unsafe or overstimulating.
- Hand and prop continuity are unclear.
- The emotional and camera direction are missing.
- The action is difficult to review or regenerate locally.
- The ending state is undefined.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Segment 1: Lumi holds the red ball and turns her upper body gently toward the red basket.

Segment 2: Lumi takes two slow steps toward the basket while holding the ball with both hands.

Segment 3: Lumi bends slightly and places the ball inside.

Keep the camera locked, movement slow, expression calm, and screen direction unchanged. Do not add waving, jumping, spinning, throwing, or extra gestures.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Using every gesture at once.
2. Making hands cross the face.
3. Allowing uncontrolled full-body motion.
4. Throwing instead of placing.
5. Changing screen direction accidentally.
6. Forgetting which hand holds the prop.
7. Using extreme poses for simple emotions.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Create ten approved poses and three movement sequences.

For each pose, define:
- feet or base;
- torso;
- hands or wings;
- head;
- eyes;
- expression;
- purpose.

For each movement:
- write starting state;
- write ending state;
- identify one action;
- define camera;
- list unchanged elements;
- add safety and continuity checks.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

What should one pose communicate?

### Answer

One clear story or emotional idea.
### Question 2

Why use one movement per segment?

### Answer

It improves clarity, continuity, review, and local regeneration.
### Question 3

Why track hand occupancy?

### Answer

To preserve prop continuity and avoid impossible gestures.
### Question 4

Should movement participation be mandatory?

### Answer

No. Offer alternatives.
### Question 5

What can become a shared boundary frame?

### Answer

The stable ending pose of one segment and starting pose of the next.
