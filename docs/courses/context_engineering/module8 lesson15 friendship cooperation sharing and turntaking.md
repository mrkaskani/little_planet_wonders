# Module 8

## Lesson 15 of 72 — Friendship, Cooperation, Sharing, and Turn-Taking

**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes  
**Estimated practice time:** 15 minutes

---

# 1. Learning objective

- Design healthy peer relationships through visible action and simple dialogue.
- Show cooperation without making one character superior.
- Teach sharing and turn-taking without forced surrender.
- Create Relationship Rules.

---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Children under five learn social behavior partly by watching relationships. A friendship should not depend on one character always being correct and the other always failing. Cooperation should show listening, choice, mutual respect, and clear action.

Sharing is often oversimplified as “give the object away immediately.” Young children may instead need help understanding ownership, waiting, asking, taking turns, using another object, or completing a short turn. The story should avoid humiliation, forced affection, or an adult demanding instant emotional compliance.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Equal dignity

Characters may have different abilities and roles, but each remains respected. Do not make Pip permanently less capable so Lumi can teach him.

## 2. Cooperation

Cooperation means working toward a shared goal with visible contributions. One character may hold the basket while another places the ball inside.

## 3. Turn-taking

A turn has a clear beginning, duration, ending, and transition. Use simple language such as “Lumi’s turn, then Pip’s turn.”

## 4. Sharing versus giving up

Sharing may mean using together, dividing materials, taking turns, or offering a choice. It should not always require immediate surrender of a personally held object.

## 5. Asking first

Characters should ask before taking another character’s object. Model clear phrases: “May I use the red crayon when you are finished?”

## 6. Waiting support

Waiting becomes easier when the child knows what happens next. Use visible order, a short song, another activity, or a clear time marker.

## 7. Mutual help

Helping should respect autonomy. Ask, “Would you like help?” rather than taking over.

## 8. No forced affection

Do not require hugging, touching, or saying “I love you” to prove repair or friendship. Offer verbal and non-contact alternatives.

## 9. Celebrating cooperation

Recognize the process: listening, waiting, asking, and working together. Avoid ranking one child as smarter or better.

## 10. Relationship continuity

Track trust, shared experiences, known preferences, and approved growth. A resolved disagreement should not be repeated identically without reason.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

Lumi is using the red crayon.

**Pip:** “May I use the red crayon?”

**Lumi:** “I am drawing one more flower. Then it is your turn.”

Pip chooses the blue crayon while waiting.

Lumi finishes and places the red crayon in the center of the table.

**Lumi:** “I am finished. Your turn, Pip.”

**Pip:** “Thank you.”

No one is forced to give up the crayon immediately, and the turn transition is clear.

# 7. Weak prompt

> Pip grabs Lumi’s crayon, Lumi gets angry, Auntie Noura forces Lumi to share, and they hug.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- Grabbing is shown before safe requesting.
- Lumi’s ownership and current turn are ignored.
- Forced sharing may teach that boundaries do not matter.
- Forced hugging treats physical affection as mandatory.
- The adult controls the solution rather than guiding communication.
- Emotional escalation is unnecessary.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Pip asks, “May I use the red crayon when you are finished?”

Lumi says, “I am drawing one more flower. Then it is your turn.”

Pip chooses another crayon while waiting. Lumi completes the flower and places the red crayon where Pip can reach it.

Auntie Noura says, “You asked, waited, and took turns.”

Do not include grabbing, shouting, forced sharing, punishment, or forced physical affection.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Treating sharing as immediate surrender.
2. Making one character always correct.
3. Using adults to force compliance.
4. Skipping the waiting period.
5. Using apology or affection as punishment.
6. Celebrating one character as better.
7. Repeating the same conflict without continuity.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Write a turn-taking scene.

1. Choose one shared object.
2. Define who has the current turn.
3. Write the request.
4. Write the response and visible limit.
5. Give the waiting character a safe option.
6. Show the turn ending.
7. Show the transition.
8. Add one process-focused recognition line.
9. Review for coercion, shame, and forced touch.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

Does sharing always mean giving an object away immediately?

### Answer

No. It may mean taking turns, dividing, using together, or choosing another option.
### Question 2

Why define a turn ending?

### Answer

The waiting character needs predictable information.
### Question 3

Should help be automatic?

### Answer

No. Ask whether help is wanted when appropriate.
### Question 4

Is hugging required after conflict?

### Answer

No. Repair can be verbal or non-contact.
### Question 5

What should praise focus on?

### Answer

Listening, asking, waiting, and cooperation.
