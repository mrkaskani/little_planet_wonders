**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

# 1. Learning objective

- Create the first half of a child-friendly story structure.
- Begin episodes with familiarity and orientation.
- Design one small visible problem connected to the learning goal.
- Plan two or three safe attempts without humiliation or excessive repetition.
- Create a Beginning-and-Problem Story Template.

---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Young children understand new learning more easily when it begins inside a familiar situation. The episode should first establish who is present, where they are, and what ordinary activity is happening. A problem introduced before orientation may feel confusing or unnecessarily alarming.

The problem should be small enough for the audience to understand and safe enough to explore. Attempts should reveal information and move the story forward. They should not merely repeat failure or make a character look foolish.

The story’s first half creates the learning question: what do the characters notice, what needs to change, and what will they try?

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Familiar beginning

Start with a recognizable routine, place, relationship, or activity. Familiarity lowers cognitive load and helps the child notice the new element.

## 2. Orientation

Show where the characters are, who is present, what they are doing, and the general emotional tone before introducing a problem.

## 3. Inciting discovery

The story changes when a character notices something: a ball outside its basket, a plant without water, a missing shape, or two characters needing the same object.

## 4. Small visible problem

The problem should be concrete and shown on screen. Avoid depending on abstract explanation.

## 5. Connection to learning goal

The problem exists because the main concept matters. The red ball needs a matching basket, making color comparison necessary.

## 6. Attempt

An attempt is a character’s reasonable action to solve the problem. It should reveal information even when it does not fully succeed.

## 7. Safe failure

A failed attempt should not cause danger, shame, or loud punishment. It should clarify what the characters now know.

## 8. Number of attempts

Two or three attempts are usually enough. Too many repeated failures may frustrate the audience and slow the learning.

## 9. Escalation limits

The problem may become clearer, but not more dangerous. Avoid turning a mismatch into a chase, emergency, or emotional crisis.

## 10. Participation placement

A participation moment often works after the first useful attempt, when the child has enough information to help.

## 11. Character roles

One character may discover, another may guess, and a guide may support. Avoid making the guide deliver the answer immediately.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

## First half of *The Red Ball Finds a Home*

### Familiar beginning

Lumi and Pip enter the Rainbow Garden to put away play materials.

### Discovery

Lumi notices one red ball on the path.

**Lumi:** “A red ball is not in its basket.”

### Small problem

They need to find where the ball belongs.

### Attempt 1

Pip looks at the blue basket.

**Pip:** “Does it go here?”

Lumi compares the colors.

**Lumi:** “The ball is red. This basket is blue.”

### New information

They know the blue basket does not match.

### Preparation for participation

The red basket is now visible beside the blue basket.

The story has a clear learning question without danger, humiliation, or unnecessary complexity.

# 6. Reusable prompt template

Write the first half of a preschool story using this structure:

**Permanent context**  
Audience profile, Series Bible, Child-Safety Bible, Emotional-Safety Guide, Character Bibles, World Bible, and educational goal.

1. Begin with a familiar location, relationship, and activity.
2. Orient the child clearly.
3. Introduce one visible discovery.
4. Create one small problem connected to the learning goal.
5. Plan two or three safe attempts at most.
6. Make every attempt reveal useful information.
7. Avoid danger, humiliation, shouting, punishment, or escalating suspense.
8. Prepare one participation opportunity after enough information is visible.

**Output requirements**  
Provide story beats, exact problem statement, attempt results, and emotional-safety review.

---

# 7. Weak prompt

> Start with a big emergency, make the characters fail many times, increase the danger, and ask the child to save them.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- The child receives no familiar orientation.
- The problem intensity is too high.
- Repeated failure may become frustrating or humiliating.
- Danger is used to create engagement.
- The child is pressured to rescue the characters.
- The learning goal may disappear behind suspense.
- No safe attempt or useful information pattern is defined.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Begin with Lumi and Pip putting away safe garden play materials in the familiar Rainbow Garden.

Lumi notices one red ball on the path. The small problem is finding its matching basket. Pip first considers the blue basket. This attempt reveals that red and blue do not match.

Show the red and blue baskets clearly, then invite the child to help identify the red basket. Keep the problem mild, the characters calm, and every attempt useful. Do not add danger, urgency, repeated failure, or rescue pressure.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Starting with conflict before orientation.
2. Making the problem too abstract.
3. Using many repeated failures.
4. Escalating mild problems into emergencies.
5. Letting the adult answer immediately.
6. Making one character look foolish.
7. Asking the child before showing enough information.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Create the first half of one episode.

1. Define the familiar beginning.
2. Orient the child to character, place, and activity.
3. Introduce one discovery.
4. State one small problem.
5. Plan two attempts at most.
6. Write the result and new information from each attempt.
7. Prepare one participation question.
8. Review the beats for safety, clarity, and emotional comfort.
