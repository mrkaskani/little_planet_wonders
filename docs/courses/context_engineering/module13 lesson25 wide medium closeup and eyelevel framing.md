---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Framing controls what the child can see and understand. A wide shot explains where characters are. A medium shot supports conversation and gesture. A close-up shows emotion or an important object.

Using the wrong framing can hide information. A close-up may show Lumi’s face but hide the basket choices. A wide shot may show the whole garden but make the red ball too small to identify.

For children under five, the camera should usually help orientation, not create visual excitement through extreme angles or constant changes.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Wide shot

Shows characters and location together. Use it to establish where the action occurs, show safe boundaries, or display a complete body action.

## 2. Medium shot

Shows enough of the body for gesture and interaction while keeping faces readable. It is useful for dialogue, object showing, and participation.

## 3. Close-up

Shows face or object detail. Use it when emotion, pronunciation, or one learning detail matters. Avoid extreme close-ups that distort or frighten.

## 4. Object close-up

A controlled close view of the learning object. It should preserve orientation and return smoothly to character context.

## 5. Eye-level framing

The camera is approximately at the character’s eye height. It feels direct, stable, and respectful.

## 6. High angle

Looking down may make a character appear smaller or show layout. Use gently and only when it helps understanding.

## 7. Low angle

Looking up may make a figure appear powerful or threatening. It is rarely needed in the example series.

## 8. Framing for participation

The child must see the questioner and answer choices. A medium-wide or carefully composed medium shot is often better than a close-up.

## 9. Headroom and body visibility

Leave comfortable space around the character. Do not crop hands when the gesture or held prop matters.

## 10. Shot-size continuity

Move between sizes for a reason. Establish the scene before using detail, and return to spatial context when necessary.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

## Red-ball sequence

1. **Wide shot:** Lumi and Pip enter the Rainbow Garden. The path and baskets are visible.
2. **Medium shot:** Lumi notices the ball and points.
3. **Object close-up:** The red ball is clearly shown.
4. **Medium-wide participation shot:** Lumi holds the ball while red and blue baskets are visible.
5. **Medium shot:** Lumi places the ball inside the red basket.
6. **Close-up:** Lumi smiles gently during the recap.

Each shot size has a clear purpose.

# 6. Reusable prompt template

Choose the correct framing for a preschool shot.

**Current story purpose**  
[Orient, speak, show emotion, demonstrate object, invite participation]

**Starting state**  
[Characters, location, prop]

**Ending state**  
[What must be understood]

Select:
- wide;
- medium-wide;
- medium;
- close-up;
- object close-up.

Define:
- camera height;
- distance;
- what remains in frame;
- what may be cropped;
- how the shot connects to the previous and next framing.

**Child-audience requirements**  
Preserve orientation, facial readability, object visibility, safety boundaries, and comfortable perspective.

**Output requirements**  
One framing decision with a reason.

# 7. Weak prompt

> Use dramatic extreme close-ups and low angles for every important moment.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- Extreme close-ups can distort faces.
- Low angles may make characters feel threatening.
- Location and object context disappear.
- Repeated dramatic framing creates unnecessary intensity.
- The framing does not respond to different learning purposes.
- Participation choices may be hidden.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Use eye-level framing as the default. Begin with a wide shot to establish the Rainbow Garden. Use a medium shot when Lumi notices the ball, an object close-up to identify red, and a medium-wide shot for the participation question so both baskets remain visible.

Reserve close-up for the final gentle emotional response. Do not use extreme close-ups, low-angle power framing, or dramatic distortion.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Using a close-up before establishing location.
2. Making the learning object too small.
3. Cropping hands during a gesture.
4. Using low angles for excitement.
5. Using a wide shot for detailed emotion.
6. Changing sizes constantly.
7. Forgetting to return from object detail to character context.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Create a six-shot framing plan for one scene.

For each shot:
1. State shot size.
2. State camera height.
3. State purpose.
4. List what must remain visible.
5. Define transition from the previous shot.
6. Review child orientation and comfort.

What does a wide shot communicate?

### Answer

Dialogue, gestures, and object showing.
