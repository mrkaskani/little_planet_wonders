# 2. Why it matters for children under five

Color and lighting can change how a scene feels before a character speaks. Warm soft light may feel safe. Strong contrast, dark shadows, red highlights, or sudden light changes may create danger even when the story problem is mild.

A color script maps emotional and visual progression across an episode. It is not merely a list of colors. It shows how dominant color, contrast, brightness, and light direction support each story beat.

For children under five, difficult moments should remain readable and familiar. Lighting may become slightly cooler or quieter during uncertainty, but should not hide faces, paths, adults, or safety boundaries.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Color script

A sequence showing how palette, contrast, brightness, and accent change across scenes according to learning and emotion.

## 2. Baseline lighting

Every series needs a normal safe lighting state. For Lumi and Pip, it is soft warm daylight or warm indoor light with visible faces and gentle shadows.

## 3. Emotional modulation

A mild problem may use slightly cooler or more neutral surroundings, while identity colors remain stable.

## 4. Contrast limits

High contrast can make scenes dramatic or frightening. Keep shadow detail visible and avoid black areas that suggest hidden threat.

## 5. Light direction

Stable direction supports continuity. Sudden reversal may make faces and locations feel inconsistent.

## 6. Night safety

Night can remain blue, soft, and readable. Use practical warm lights, visible caregivers, and calm ambience. Avoid deep darkness and unknown silhouettes.

## 7. Weather lighting

Rain may use soft overcast light without storm darkness, lightning, or harsh reflections.

## 8. Learning emphasis

Light may gently guide attention toward an object, but should not create unnatural spotlight effects that break the visual world.

## 9. Transition planning

If time or weather changes, show a clear transition. Do not jump from warm morning to dark evening between connected shots.

## 10. Generated-light review

AI video may introduce flicker, exposure breathing, color shifts, or unstable shadows. Review the actual clip.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

## Color script for The Red Ball Finds a Home

### Scene 1 — Welcome
- Warm amber daylight.
- Low contrast.
- Calm garden palette.

### Scene 2 — Discovery
- Neutral warm light.
- Red ball receives slightly stronger emphasis.
- Curiosity, not alarm.

### Scene 3 — First mismatch
- Slightly cooler neutral surroundings.
- Faces remain warm and clear.
- No darkness.

### Scene 4 — Participation
- Balanced light.
- Red and blue choices clearly separated.
- Background simplified.

### Scene 5 — Solution
- Warmth returns.
- Soft golden accent.
- Calm celebration.

The script creates emotional progression without making the mild mismatch feel dangerous.

# 6. Reusable prompt template

Create a safe emotional color script and lighting plan for a preschool episode.

Use:
- approved Color Bible;
- Location Bible;
- emotional goals;
- safety rules;
- story beats.

For each scene, define:
- emotional state;
- dominant color;
- accent;
- brightness;
- contrast;
- light direction;
- character and object visibility;
- transition from the previous scene.

**Elements allowed to change**  
Environmental color balance, temporary accent, time, and approved weather.

**Elements that must remain unchanged**  
Character identity colors, prop identity, location layout, safety visibility, and overall visual medium.

**Child-safety requirements**  
No flashing, deep darkness, threatening silhouette, harsh color wash, or sudden exposure change.

**Review criteria**  
The emotional shift must remain proportional to the story problem.

---

# 7. Weak prompt

> When Pip chooses the wrong basket, make the scene dark red with deep shadows and dramatic flashing light.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- The visual reaction is disproportionate to a mild mistake.
- Dark red may suggest danger or punishment.
- Deep shadows may hide expressions and boundaries.
- Flashing creates sensory and safety concerns.
- The style contradicts the series emotional promise.
- The prompt may shame the mistake through visual punishment.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

During the first mismatch, keep the Rainbow Garden clearly visible. Shift the surrounding light slightly toward a neutral soft tone while preserving warm readable faces and stable character colors.

Use low-to-medium contrast, soft shadows, and no flashing. Keep the red ball and blue basket clearly distinguishable. When Lumi says, “That is okay. We can look again,” gently restore the normal warm balance.

The lighting should communicate brief uncertainty, not danger or punishment.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Using darkness for every problem.
2. Changing identity colors for emotion.
3. Creating harsh spotlight effects.
4. Making night scenes unreadable.
5. Using lightning for visual excitement.
6. Changing light direction between connected shots.
7. Reviewing the prompt but not the generated light.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

What is a color script?

### Answer

A scene-by-scene plan for palette, brightness, contrast, and emotional progression.
### Question 2

Should a mild mistake use danger lighting?

### Answer

No.
### Question 3

Why preserve face visibility?

### Answer

Children need to read emotion and safety.
### Question 4

Can night be used?

### Answer

Yes, if it remains soft, clear, and reassuring.
### Question 5

Why review generated light?

### Answer

Video may introduce flicker, exposure changes, and unstable shadows.
