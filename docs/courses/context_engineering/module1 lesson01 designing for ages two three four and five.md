# Module 1

## Lesson 1 of 72 — Designing for Ages Two, Three, Four, and Five

**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes  
**Estimated practice time:** 15 minutes

---

# 1. Learning objective

- Describe practical developmental differences between children around ages two, three, four, and five.
- Choose a central design age instead of treating the entire two-to-five range as one identical audience.
- Translate audience needs into language, pacing, story, emotion, participation, visual, and audio rules.
- Create the first draft of a Target Audience Profile.

---

# 2. Why it matters for children under five

A video can be attractive and technically polished while still being too difficult for its audience. A two-year-old may understand a visible object and one simple action but lose the meaning of a long verbal explanation. A five-year-old may follow a short reasoning sequence, remember an earlier clue, and compare several choices. If a creator writes for “all preschool children” without a central design level, the result often becomes inconsistent: one scene is extremely simple, the next scene contains long dialogue, and another scene depends on abstract reasoning.

Audience design is therefore permanent project context. It influences every later decision: how many characters appear, how long a sentence may be, how many story attempts are acceptable, how quickly a camera may move, how long a child needs to answer, how strong an emotion may become, and how much information may appear in one frame.


## Recurring example series

Throughout these lessons, examples come from **Lumi and Pip’s Little Garden**, a gentle animated series for children approximately two to five, designed primarily for ages three to four.

- **Lumi:** a curious four-year-old child who learns by observing, asking questions, and trying again.
- **Pip:** a small teal garden bird who notices patterns and invites audience participation.
- **Auntie Noura:** a calm adult guide who models safe behavior without taking over every problem.
- **Main locations:** the Rainbow Garden, the Cozy Workshop, and the Little Pond Path.
- **Visual identity:** rounded 2D cut-paper shapes, soft texture, readable silhouettes, simple backgrounds, warm daylight, and stable eye-level cameras.
- **Audio identity:** short dialogue, warm voices, restrained music, gentle Foley, quiet observation space, and no sudden loud sounds.

# 3. Core concepts

## 1. Developmental ranges, not rigid labels

Children develop at different rates. Age guidance should help creators design understandable content, not diagnose individual children or declare what every child “must” be able to do. Use phrases such as “many children around age three may benefit from…” rather than absolute claims.

## 2. Designing around age two

Prioritize concrete nouns, familiar verbs, very short sentences, visible cause and effect, one important object, one clear action, and immediate reassurance. Participation is strongest when children can point, name, imitate one gentle movement, or choose between two obvious options. Avoid relying on long memory, indirect humor, or multi-step verbal instructions.

## 3. Designing around age three

Children may follow a slightly longer sequence: a familiar beginning, a small problem, one attempt, a helpful clue, and a safe solution. Dialogue can include short exchanges and simple descriptive words. Participation may include naming, completing a repeated phrase, or choosing between two or three clearly visible options.

## 4. Designing around age four

A four-year-old audience may follow a clear goal, two or three attempts, a simple rule, and a short recap. Characters may name mild frustration, ask for help, and try another strategy. Dialogue can explain a simple visible reason, but it should not turn into an adult lecture.

## 5. Designing around age five

Children may follow short planning sequences, remember an earlier clue, compare multiple choices, and discuss simple social reasoning. Even so, dialogue should remain concrete and visually supported. “Older preschool” does not mean adult pacing or dense explanation.

## 6. Central design age

A project may be accessible across ages two to five while still choosing one central design level. For Lumi and Pip, the central design level is approximately three to four. Younger viewers can understand the visual action; older viewers receive optional prediction or reasoning opportunities.

## 7. Layered accessibility

One moment can work at several levels. A younger child sees a red ball. Another child hears the label “red.” Another repeats the word. An older child decides which basket matches. The story continues even when the child does not answer.

## 8. Accessibility and caregiver perspective

Important information should be communicated through more than one channel when possible: clear visuals, spoken words, object position, and repeated action. Participation should allow speaking, pointing, moving, or simply watching. Caregivers should be able to trust the content’s language level, emotional tone, safety, accuracy, and respectful treatment of children.

# 4. Practical example from the ongoing series

### Weak version

Lumi enters a crowded garden, sees five objects, explains primary and secondary colors, argues with Pip about storage, and sorts everything within twenty seconds.

This version overloads the audience with objects, concepts, dialogue, movement, and conflict.

### Improved version

Lumi notices one red ball and stops.

**Lumi:** “A red ball.”

Pip lands beside her.

**Pip:** “Where does it belong?”

Lumi shows one blue basket and one red basket.

**Lumi:** “Can you find the red basket?”

The camera stays still while Lumi waits.

**Pip:** “There it is!”

Lumi places the ball inside.

**Lumi:** “Red ball. Red basket. They match.”

This version presents one concept, one visible problem, limited choices, repetition, a real pause, and a clear solution.

# 7. Weak prompt

> Make a fun, exciting, colorful cartoon for all children aged two to five.

---

# 8. Diagnosis of the weak prompt

- No central design age is defined.
- The learning and emotional purposes are missing.
- “Exciting” may be interpreted as fast editing, loud audio, or chaotic motion.
- “For all children” ignores differences in language, attention, sensory needs, and experience.
- No safety, accessibility, participation, or caregiver requirements are provided.

---

# 9. Improved prompt

Develop the audience direction for a warm recurring animated series designed primarily for children aged three to four, visually understandable to many two-year-olds, and offering optional prediction for many five-year-olds.

Use short concrete sentences, one primary learning idea, one mild problem, two or three attempts at most, and clear visual cause and effect. Present one main object or action during learning moments. Invite pointing, naming, repeating, choosing between two options, or watching. Pause without pressure and continue gently even when the child does not respond.

Allow curiosity, mild uncertainty, gentle frustration, relief, and reassurance. Avoid prolonged fear, humiliation, aggressive conflict, threatening voices, sudden loud sounds, rapid cutting, flashing effects, and unsafe imitation.

Create a Target Audience Profile that directly guides dialogue, story, visuals, voice, music, sound, editing, educational review, and child-safety review.

# 12. Common mistakes

1. Treating ages two to five as one identical audience.
2. Using constant speed to hold attention.
3. Teaching several unrelated ideas in one short episode.
4. Asking questions without response time.
5. Treating participation as a test.
6. Assuming every child can perform the same movement.
7. Using adult-style explanations because the topic is educational.

---

# 13. Fifteen-minute practical exercise

Create a one-page Target Audience Profile.

1. Choose the primary range, central design age, and wider accessible range.
2. Define typical sentence length and maximum essential new vocabulary.
3. Define the number of main ideas and attempts in one short episode.
4. Select three participation methods.
5. Define allowed emotional intensity.
6. Add one visual-comfort rule and one audio-comfort rule.
7. Add two accessibility supports and two caregiver expectations.

---

# 14. Expected exercise result

A completed profile that can guide production decisions. It should be specific enough that two different writers would produce work at a similar developmental level.

# 15. Short quiz and answers

### Question 1

Why choose a central design age?

### Answer

To guide language, story, pacing, participation, emotion, and audiovisual complexity.

### Question 2

What is layered accessibility?

### Answer

A moment that supports several levels of understanding without requiring every child to perform the same response.

### Question 3

What is usually more accessible to a younger child: abstract explanation or visible cause and effect?

### Answer

Visible cause and effect.

### Question 4

Should the story stop when the child does not answer?

### Answer

No. Participation is invited, not required.

### Question 5

Why are developmental descriptions treated as ranges?

### Answer

Because children develop differently and no age description applies rigidly to every child.
