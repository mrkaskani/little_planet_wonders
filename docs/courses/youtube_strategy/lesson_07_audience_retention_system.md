# LittlePlanetWonders Growth Course

## Lesson 7 of 16: Audience-Retention System

**Study time:** 15 minutes  
**Practice time:** 15 minutes

---

## 1. Lesson objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Design retention without manipulation or overstimulation.
- Structure a 30-second Short, 3-minute lesson, 6–8-minute video, and 20-minute compilation.
- Use questions, repetition, story, music, and visual changes intentionally.
- Diagnose retention dips and spikes.
- Build a reusable retention map before production.

---

## 2. Retention is not constant excitement

Retention means the child has a reason to continue because the experience remains understandable, interesting, and rewarding.

It does not require:

- Constant shouting.
- A visual cut every second.
- Artificial danger.
- Endless surprises.
- Loud music.
- Rapid random movement.

For LittlePlanetWonders, healthy retention should come from:

- Curiosity.
- Participation.
- Prediction.
- Meaningful repetition.
- Character relationships.
- A clear learning goal.
- A satisfying resolution.

---

## 3. The retention rhythm

A useful preschool rhythm is:

1. **Question or problem**
2. **Model**
3. **Child response**
4. **Reveal or reward**
5. **Small change**
6. **Repeat at a slightly higher level**

Example:

- “Which animal is hiding?”
- Show a tail and model two choices.
- Pause for the child.
- Reveal the cow.
- Move to a new part of the farm.
- Ask the child to remember the word later.

---

## 4. The first 3, 10, and 30 seconds

### First 3 seconds

Purpose: communicate visual interest and context.

Examples:

- A missing object.
- A surprising but safe character mistake.
- A colorful clue.
- A clear question.

### First 10 seconds

Purpose: explain the mission.

> “Luna lost five colors in the garden. Can you help bring them back?”

### First 30 seconds

Purpose: begin the learning activity and complete the first small reward.

The first target word should usually appear before the viewer has waited through a long introduction.

---

## 5. Structure for a 30-second Short

### 0–2 seconds: Hook

> “Which animal says moo?”

### 2–8 seconds: Choices

Show two or three clear animals.

### 8–15 seconds: Thinking pause

Repeat the question and give response time.

### 15–22 seconds: Reveal and model

> “The cow! A cow says moo.”

### 22–28 seconds: Quick recall

> “Which animal says moo?”

### 28–30 seconds: Natural loop

Return visually to the opening choice.

The Short must still deliver a complete learning result rather than functioning only as an advertisement.

---

## 6. Structure for a 3-minute lesson

### 0:00–0:15 — Immediate objective

Introduce the mission and first target.

### 0:15–1:00 — First two items

Model, repeat, and recognize.

### 1:00–1:15 — Movement or rhythm reset

Use a short action connected to the words.

### 1:15–2:15 — Next items and challenge

Increase difficulty slightly by hiding or comparing items.

### 2:15–2:45 — Memory game

Ask the child to recall several items.

### 2:45–3:00 — Recap and next adventure

Review the objective and create a gentle continuation cue.

---

## 7. Structure for a 6–8-minute educational video

### 0:00–0:30 — Problem and first success

Do not spend all 30 seconds explaining.

### 0:30–2:00 — First learning cycle

Teach two target items.

### 2:00–2:30 — Participation reset

Movement, sound imitation, or a simple prediction.

### 2:30–4:30 — Second learning cycle

Teach two or three more items with variation.

### 4:30–5:30 — Story complication

Use a small, safe challenge that requires the target language.

### 5:30–6:30 — Recognition and recall

Let the child choose and remember.

### 6:30–7:30 — Resolution and recap

Resolve the story and revisit the objective.

### Final seconds — Related next episode

Use a spoken and visual cue, especially because some interactive features may be limited for made-for-kids content.

---

## 8. Structure for a 20-minute compilation

A compilation should not be a random group of clips.

### Recommended design

- One theme, such as animals or daily routines.
- Three or four related segments.
- A short host transition between segments.
- A mini recap after each segment.
- A complete recap at the end.

Example:

1. Farm animals
2. Forest animals
3. Ocean animals
4. Final animal guessing game

Avoid repeating long logos and introductions between every reused segment.

---

## 9. Pacing and visual changes

A visual change should support one of these functions:

- Reveal new meaning.
- Refocus attention.
- Show a response.
- Move to the next learning stage.
- Demonstrate an action.

It should not happen only because a fixed number of seconds has passed.

Useful changes:

- Character expression
- Camera distance
- Object appearance
- Location section
- On-screen word
- Gesture

Keep target objects visible long enough for children to identify them.

---

## 10. Music and sound effects

### Use music to

- Establish warmth and curiosity.
- Mark a transition.
- Support a chant.
- Provide gentle anticipation.

### Reduce or remove music when

- A new word is pronounced.
- The child is expected to answer.
- A subtle sound difference is being taught.
- Dialogue becomes difficult to understand.

### Use sound effects to

- Confirm a correct reveal.
- Represent an animal or action.
- Highlight a clue.

Avoid loud reward sounds after every response. Learning should remain the main reward.

## 11. Diagnose retention data

Official YouTube Analytics includes audience-retention reports at the video level. Platform documentation and metrics can change, so verify the latest official guidance.

### Early dip

Possible causes:

- Long introduction
- Slow opening
- Thumbnail promise not delivered
- Audio or visual confusion

### Dip during explanation

Possible causes:

- Sentences are too difficult
- Too much adult-style explanation
- Not enough visual demonstration

### Spike

Possible causes:

- Viewers rewatched a useful word
- A funny or surprising moment
- A confusing section required replay

Do not automatically interpret every spike as success. Check what happens in the scene.

### Stable section

Possible causes:

- Strong participation
- Clear story progress
- Effective repetition

Reuse the structure, not necessarily the exact content.

## 12. Retention map template

Create this before animation:

| Time | Story event | Learning event | Child action | Visual change | Audio purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Problem appears | Objective introduced | Look and listen | Empty basket | Music stops for question |
| 0:10 | First clue | Word 1 | Repeat | Close-up object | Clear voice |
| 0:35 | Choice | Recognition | Point or answer | Two options | Thinking pause |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

A retention map helps identify long sections where the child has no action and no new story development.

## 13. Practice

Create a retention map for:

> **Colors in the Magic Garden**

Requirements:

- Five target colors.
- At least three child-response pauses.
- One movement activity.
- One prediction.
- One memory question.
- One final recap.
- No scene change that exists only for stimulation.

## 14. Quiz

1. What should retention be based on for LittlePlanetWonders?
2. What should happen within the first 30 seconds?
3. Why should music be reduced during pronunciation?
4. What can a retention spike mean besides success?
5. Why is a compilation more than a group of clips?

---

## 15. Quiz answers

1. Curiosity, participation, prediction, meaningful repetition, story progress, and learning value.
2. The mission should be clear and the first learning action should begin.
3. So the child can hear the word or sound clearly.
4. The section may be confusing and viewers may be replaying it.
5. It needs thematic organization, transitions, and a learning progression.
